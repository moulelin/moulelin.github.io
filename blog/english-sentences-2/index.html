had better
1. Means "should; had best";
You had better study hard for your entrance exam.
You had better see the doctor about your cough.
2. It is followed by the infinitive without to: had better do sth;
you had better be careful.
It’s five o’clock. I’d better go now before the traffic gets too bad.
3. It is used like a modal verb; had does not change for person or number.
She’d better get here soon or she’ll miss the opening ceremony.
4. It is often used to make a fairly strong suggestion, usually stressing that there will be bad consequences if you don't do it.
It can also express an urgent wish, a warning, etc.
We'd better take something to eat or we will be hungry later.
You had better lock the door before you leave.
You had better unplug the toaster before you try to clean it.

Passive voice

The active voice means the subject of the sentence is the doer or performer of the action. The passive voice means the subject of the sentence is the receiver of the action. If you need to show who performs the action, use by; the predicate takes the form "the appropriate tense of be + past participle".
Don't worry if that is hard to follow; an example will make it clear.
[Example] The passive voice in the past tense
Active: The teacher gave me the book.
Passive: I was given the book by the teacher.
From this example we can see that the teacher gave me a book: the teacher is the doer of the action and I am the receiver. So in the active voice, the subject, verb and object appear in that order.
In the passive voice, however, the object is moved to the front of the sentence, so the object now takes on the action and the predicate has to change accordingly: gave becomes was given. (Memory aid: in the passive voice, simple present is/are + done, simple past was/were + done, simple future shall/will be + done.)
[Example] The passive voice in the present perfect tense
Active: We have finished our work.
Passive: Our work has been finished.
(Memory aid: in the passive voice, present perfect has/have been + done, past perfect had been + done, future perfect shall/will have been + done.)
At this point, you may have noticed that have been done appears again. It has exactly the same pattern as have been married in today's key sentence, They have been married for more than 10 years. So is the key sentence passive?
The answer is no. This is a point that deserves special attention once you have learned the passive voice.
● "be + past participle" is not necessarily the passive voice
[Examples]
The glass plate is broken. The glass plate is broken. (predicative)
The glass plate was broken by him. The glass plate was broken by him (at that time). (passive voice)
[Examples]
The book is written well. The book is well written. (predicative)
The book has been written by our teacher. The book has been written by our teacher. (passive voice)
Let's analyse today's key sentence.
They have been married for more than 10 years.
Put briefly, "they are married":
They are married.
"They have (already) been married":
They have been married.
So today's key sentence uses a predicative structure; it is not the passive voice.

how come: why; how could this happen

how come is an expression commonly used by Americans, meaning "how could it be" or "why is it so". Native speakers don't use how come to ask how someone got somewhere; for that we can say: how do you get here.

how come means basically the same as why, but there are a few differences.

① how come is generally used when something has happened. If someone says how come to you, it means they don't understand how it happened and really want your explanation. Compared with why, how come can express curiosity and shock, so its tone is stronger.

② Also, how come is more colloquial and is mostly used in everyday conversation, whereas why can be used in both writing and speech, so its range of use is wider.

③ There is one more very important difference that you must remember: why is followed by question word order, but how come is followed by statement word order. Don't get this wrong.
